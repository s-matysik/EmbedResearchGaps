# Response to reviewers

**Manuscript SOFTX-D-26-01416** — "EmbedResearchGaps: embedding-based shortlisting and stability assessment of candidate research gaps in author-keyword space"

The revision is supplied in two forms for both the manuscript and the supplementary material: a marked copy, in which every passage that differs from the submitted version carries a yellow background and each figure caption states whether the figure was regenerated, and a clean copy with no marking. We sincerely thank the Editor and all three Reviewers for their careful evaluation of our manuscript and for their constructive recommendations. Their comments have improved the methodological clarity, the presentation and the interpretation of the study, and we have considered each point in detail, indicating below the corresponding change in the revised manuscript and supplementary material. Three comments in particular changed the paper rather than its wording: the absence of a comparison against simpler shortlists (Reviewer 1, opening remark; Reviewer 2, points 16, 17 and 22; Reviewer 3, third remark), the absence of a power analysis and of rank-aware stability measures (Reviewer 2, points 9 and 10), and the numerical inconsistencies listed in Reviewer 2's point 12, of which six were genuine defects that we have corrected and traced to their source.

Because the panel had annotated every author keyword of each corpus, not only the candidates, the comparison the reviewers asked for could be computed on the existing judgements without new annotation. The result is reported in the new Section 3.4: the ranking does not outperform a random draw from the rare keywords its own filters admit. We have taken this as the occasion to reframe the contribution as Reviewer 2 suggests in point 17, towards what the evidence supports.

Four further analyses were run for this revision and are reported in new supplementary sections: an ablation of the composite-score weights (S10b), a sensitivity analysis of the frequency ceiling (S10c), rank-aware stability measures with their methods and limits stated (S6c), and a paired re-annotation of the same keywords with the corpus evidence supplied in the prompt (S18), which Reviewer 2 proposed in point 8 and which we had previously judged out of scope. We also added a test module that pins every parameter quoted in the article to the code and to the deposited run configurations, so that the class of inconsistency Reviewer 2 found in point 12 now fails the test suite rather than reaching a reader; the suite stands at 407 tests.

Below, every numbered comment is quoted verbatim and answered in order. Changes in the manuscript are marked by section; changes in the supplementary material by section number. A summary of all changes closes this letter.

## Reviewer 1

Dear Reviewer 1, thank you for a review that identified both the main evidential gap in the submission and a set of presentation defects we had not seen. The requested comparison with simpler alternatives has been added as a new section, all five numbered points are addressed below, and Figures 1, 2 and 4 have been regenerated.

**Opening remark.** *"Although I appreciate the authors' transparency in reporting negative results, I believe that the practical benefits of the proposed tool should be demonstrated more clearly in comparison with simpler alternative approaches, such as lexical filtering, random selection, or semantic similarity-based methods without clustering. A more comprehensive comparison would help establish the added value of the software and better justify its application in literature review processes."*

We sincerely thank the Reviewer for this recommendation; acting on it changed what the paper reports. We have added **Section 3.4, "Comparison with simpler shortlists"**. Because the five-model panel annotated every author keyword of each analysis corpus, and not only the candidates, any shortlist of the same size can be scored on exactly the same judgements with no further annotation. We compared the ranking against three alternatives: a random draw of *k* keywords from the whole population; a random draw restricted to the rare, multi-token stratum that the filters admit (the rarity-matched baseline); and the *k* most frequent keywords. Lexical filtering alone was already isolated in the filter-eligible decomposition and is retained.

The outcome is not favourable to the ranking, and we report it as such. Mode B candidates reach GAP rates of 46.2%, 65.0% and 35.4% on the three corpora, against 47.2%, 53.7% and 43.1% for unrestricted random selection and 49.2%, 59.9% and 48.1% for the rarity-matched draw. The ranking sits at the 31st, 75th and 1st percentile of the rarity-matched distribution and below its mean in five of six corpus-mode comparisons. Choosing the most frequent keywords instead is clearly worse (42.3%, 49.2%, 26.2%), which shows that the frequency ceiling carries real signal, but the embedding-based ranking cannot be shown to improve on a random choice among the rare keywords it admits.

Consequently the practical benefit is now stated in terms the evidence supports (Sections 4 and 5): a documented, reproducible reduction of about 150 author keywords to roughly 25 typed, scored and sourced candidates with support values, plus an assessment layer that can audit any such list — not a demonstrated novelty filter.

### Methods

**(6)** *"The core algorithms are described only in prose in the main text, while the detector definitions, composite-score formulae and default parameters appear in supplementary S10. Because these constitute the scientific content of the tool, the formal definitions should be presented in the main text. The weights of the composite scores (for example 0.25/0.15/0.30/0.30 for emerging concepts), the similarity window and peak for conceptual combinations, and the 95th-percentile periphery threshold are not justified, and no ablation or sensitivity analysis is provided. Since all components are min-max normalised within the candidate set, scores are also not comparable across runs, which affects the consensus procedure."*

We are grateful for this request, because acting on it uncovered errors in our own description. The formal definitions are now in Section 2.2, and we have taken every parameter from the run configuration deposited with the results rather than from the source papers. Three values we had printed were wrong: the conceptual-combination window is (0.25, 0.65) with the similarity term peaking at 0.45, not [0.45, 0.80]; the emerging-concept score is 0.25 distance + 0.15 centrality + 0.30 TF-IDF salience + 0.30 rarity, and carries no recency term; and the core-domain filter drops keywords present in more than half the records, not 0.30 of them, which supplementary S10 had stated. The emerging-concept ceiling was likewise held fixed at one source article in every run; the proportional rule *c* = max(1, round(N/50)) is available in `GapConfig` but was not used, so the size sensitivity of Section 3.2 cannot be attributed to a moving threshold, as our earlier text implied. Section 2.2 and S10 now agree with each other and with the deposited configuration, and S10 states its provenance.

On justification, we have now run the ablation rather than argue about it. Supplementary **S10b** rescores every deposited candidate set under equal weights and under four leave-one-out variants. Because membership is decided by the filters and not by the score, the candidate set is identical in every variant and only the reading order changes: equal weights reproduce the published order at Kendall's tau 0.78-0.87, so the particular values carry little of the result, while removing the distance component moves it most (tau 0.39-0.67, top-5 overlap down to 0.4) and removing rarity least. Supplementary **S10c** does the same for the frequency ceiling, the one threshold that gates the detector: raising it from one article to two or three, or switching to the proportional rule, leaves a Jaccard overlap of 0.75-1.00 with the reported candidate set. The text now states that the weights are untuned defaults, not optima, and points to both analyses.

Your observation about normalisation is correct and is now stated in the same paragraph: because components are min-max normalised within one run's candidate set, scores order candidates within a run but are not comparable across runs. This is precisely why the consensus layer pools *membership* and not scores, which the text now says explicitly.

**(7)** *"The default configuration appears to make Mode B largely a rarity filter. With max_keyword_frequency = round(N/50), N = 50 yields a ceiling of one document, and emerging concepts are defined as keywords occurring once (Section 3.1). Because the ceiling changes with N, the consensus support across corpus sizes confounds corpus size with the filter definition. The authors are encouraged to decouple these effects (for example by using fixed absolute and relative ceilings) and to report how many candidates are singleton, multi-token keywords."*

We thank the Reviewer for asking for these counts. They are now in Section 3.1: 54–62% of Mode B candidates and 100% of Mode A candidates occur in exactly one record of the analysis corpus, and all candidates are multi-token. This is the clearest statement in the paper that Mode B is substantially a rarity filter, and Section 3.4 then shows that the ranking is not seen to outperform a rarity-matched random draw — the same finding from the other direction.

On the confound, checking it corrected us. The reported runs did **not** use the proportional ceiling: `GapConfig` defaults to a fixed ceiling of one article, and the deposited run configurations confirm that every run — including those at N = 100, 200 and 493 — used that fixed value. Our earlier text, which attributed part of the size sensitivity to a moving threshold, was therefore wrong, and Section 2.2 now states the fixed ceiling and says explicitly that the size sensitivity of Section 3.2 is not an artefact of it.

We have also run the decoupling you suggest. Supplementary **S10c** repeats both modes with the ceiling at one, two and three articles and with the proportional rule, everything else fixed: the candidate set changes very little, with a Jaccard overlap of 0.75 to 1.00 against the reported set and at most one candidate difference in size. The ceiling is not a knife-edge, and neither the candidate lists nor the instability reported in Section 3.2 are produced by it.

One correction to our own wording in this answer: Section 3.4 finds that a rarity-matched random draw is not outperformed by the ranking, which is not the same as the two performing equally; the intervals are too wide to support either statement, and the manuscript now says exactly that.

**(8)** *"The construct validity of the LLM annotation is limited. The GAP label is defined relative to what the corpus has not addressed (S8), yet the prompt does not include the corpus (Section 2.2), so scores reflect general knowledge and plausibly keyword rarity. In addition, the prefilters remove precisely the categories that the rubric labels METHOD, NOISE or LOW IMPORTANCE, which introduces a circularity that is acknowledged only in part. The authors are encouraged to test rarity as a covariate or negative control (for example the correlation of the Novelty Score with document frequency and with corpus-external frequency), to consider a variant of the prompt that supplies corpus context, and to discuss the self-preference and shared-training biases of the panel more explicitly."*

We appreciate this test being proposed, and have run it. It is reported in Section 3.3: the Spearman correlation between the Novelty Score and document frequency is −0.05, −0.03 and −0.23 across the three corpora. The judgements are therefore not a restatement of rarity — but, as the same paragraph now says, they also cannot certify a gap in the analysed corpus, because the prompt omits the corpus. Both halves of that sentence matter and both are now stated.

The circularity is stated in full rather than in part: the prefilters remove methodological, core-domain and single-token terms that the rubric itself labels METHOD, NOISE or LOW IMPORTANCE, so part of any filter-stage difference reflects agreement between the filters and the rubric rather than independent evidence. The panel's shared-training and self-preference biases are now acknowledged in Section 1 with the LLM-as-judge literature [18], and Section 5 lists the single-run nature of the panel among the limitations.

Your second suggestion we have now carried out. Supplementary **S18** repeats the annotation with a prompt identical to S8 except that each keyword is accompanied by the corpus evidence for it — how many of the fifty analysed articles use it and the titles of up to five — with an instruction that this evidence takes precedence over the model's general impression. The result is unfavourable to the instrument and we report it as such: corpus evidence does not sharpen the judgement, it inflates it. Mean Novelty Scores rise by 3.5 to 5.7 points on a ten-point scale, GAP rates rise in every arm, and of 26 label changes not one moves a keyword out of GAP. The candidate-minus-control contrast is not stable under the change either: it rises from +15.4 to +26.9 pp in management but flips from -12.5 to +8.3 pp in economics and finance. We cannot distinguish a too-strict blind prompt from an acquiescence effect in the corpus-aware one, and S18 says so. What the result does establish is that the absolute GAP rate is a property of the prompt rather than of the keyword, which is now the stated reason why Section 3.3 reports contrasts against a disjoint control and never levels.

One limitation of that study we must disclose. The paired design needs the same annotator under both prompts, and when we reran it four of the five annotators of S8 were unreachable: the Anthropic model used in the original panel is no longer served by its provider, and the xAI, DeepSeek and Google accounts returned permission-denied or HTTP 402 for lack of credits. Substituting different models would have converted a prompt comparison into a model comparison, so S18 is a single-annotator check on the one model still reachable, over 100 paired judgements, and is labelled as such. That the panel of a six-month-old study can no longer be reconstituted is itself the point of the maintenance policy we added, and of the hosted-endpoint drift reported in Section 3.6.

The remaining part we did not implement: corpus-external frequency would require a reference corpus whose licence permits redistribution of term frequencies, and we have not identified one we can deposit.

**(9)** *"No power analysis or smallest effect size of interest is provided. With 24 to 27 candidates per arm, the confidence intervals for the GAP rate span roughly ±19 pp, so the study cannot distinguish a null effect from a practically relevant one. A pre-specified minimum effect of interest (with literature or use-case justification), a minimal detectable effect and, where appropriate, equivalence testing would allow the conclusion of no reliable enrichment to be interpreted correctly. Because keywords are nested within articles and clusters, resampling at the article level (or cluster-robust inference) should also be considered."*

We sincerely thank the Reviewer for this methodological requirement. All four elements are now in Section 3.3. The minimal detectable difference at 80% power is 22–26 pp for Mode B and 49–54 pp for Mode A at the realised arm sizes. The smallest effect of interest is declared as 10 pp, justified by use: a 10 pp shift moves two to three items on a 25-item shortlist, which is the smallest change that would alter how the list is read. Equivalence testing against that margin does not reject either (smallest TOST *p* = 0.15). The conclusion is therefore stated as you suggest: the data neither demonstrate enrichment nor establish its absence, and effects below roughly 20 pp cannot be resolved by this design.

For the nesting, we implemented cluster-robust inference: the intervals were recomputed with a bootstrap that resamples article clusters rather than keywords. They widen slightly and change no conclusion — economics and finance Mode B becomes +13.0 pp with a 95% CI of −2 to 35, against −6 to 30 under the keyword bootstrap. This is reported in Section 3.3 and the per-comparison values are deposited.

We computed these on the offline `tfidf-svd` backend so that the measurement needs no credentials and no hosted endpoint, and can be rerun by any reader from the deposited corpora; this is stated in the text.

Your correction to the backend-versus-seed claim is accepted and the sentence has been rewritten. Section 3.6 now says that for Mode B the backend moves the list more than the initialisation does, and that for Mode A, where seed overlap is itself only 0.14–0.15, the two sources are comparable.
